Filter marketing is full of big percentages and vague claims. The only reliable way to know what a filter removes is **independent certification** to an ANSI/NSF standard — and, crucially, checking that the certification lists **the specific contaminant you care about**.

## The standards that matter

| Standard | What it covers | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **NSF/ANSI 42** | Aesthetic effects: chlorine, taste, odour, particulates | Pitchers, fridge filters, whole-house carbon |
| **NSF/ANSI 53** | Health effects: lead, VOCs, TTHMs, cysts, and — where listed — PFOA/PFOS | Pitchers, faucet mounts, under-sink carbon block |
| **NSF/ANSI 58** | Reverse osmosis systems: TDS plus listed contaminants like arsenic, nitrate, fluoride, PFAS, lead | Under-sink and countertop RO |
| **NSF/ANSI 401** | Emerging compounds: certain pharmaceuticals, pesticides, BPA and microplastics | Often paired with 42/53 |
| **NSF/ANSI 55** | Ultraviolet (UV) disinfection — Class A for microbiologically unsafe water | Wells, cabins, boil-water-prone areas |
| **NSF/ANSI 44** | Water softeners (cation exchange) | Whole-house softeners |
| **NSF/ANSI 62** | Distillation systems | Countertop distillers |
| **NSF/ANSI 61** | Materials safety for components that touch drinking water | Pipes, valves, filter housings |

These standards are written through the American National Standards Institute process. Products can be certified by several accredited bodies: **NSF International, the Water Quality Association (WQA Gold Seal), IAPMO, CSA Group and UL**. Any of them is fine — what matters is the listing.

## "Tested to" vs "certified to"

- **Certified to NSF/ANSI 53:** an accredited body tested the product, audits the factory, and lists it publicly. This is what you want.
- **"Tested to NSF/ANSI 53 standards"** or **"meets NSF standards":** the company may have paid a lab for a one-time test. Not the same thing.

## Verify a filter in two minutes

1. Find the model number on the box or listing.
2. Search the certifier's public database (NSF, WQA, IAPMO, CSA or UL listings).
3. Check that the listing includes **your contaminant by name** — e.g. "Lead," "PFOA/PFOS," "Arsenic V," "Nitrate."
4. Note the **rated capacity** (gallons or litres). A filter certified for lead is only certified up to that capacity.

## Matching filters to problems

| Your concern | Minimum you need |
|---|---|
| Chlorine taste or smell | NSF/ANSI 42 |
| Lead | NSF/ANSI 53 (lead) or 58 |
| PFAS (PFOA/PFOS) | NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 **with PFOA/PFOS listed** |
| Disinfection byproducts (TTHMs) | NSF/ANSI 53 (VOC/TTHM) |
| Nitrate, arsenic, fluoride, uranium | NSF/ANSI 58 (RO) with that contaminant listed |
| Bacteria, viruses | NSF/ANSI 55 Class A UV (or boil during notices) |
| Microplastics | NSF/ANSI 401 (microplastics) or a fine carbon block / RO |
| Hardness | NSF/ANSI 44 softener |

## Maintenance is half the job

A neglected filter can perform worse than no filter. Saturated carbon stops capturing contaminants, and wet media can grow bacteria. Replace cartridges at the rated capacity or time, whichever comes first; replace RO membranes per the maker's schedule; and sanitise housings when you change cartridges.

## Bottom line

Ignore the percentages on the box. Pick the standard that matches your problem, confirm the listing names your contaminant, and set a calendar reminder for cartridge changes.
