Hard water is water with a lot of dissolved **calcium and magnesium**, picked up as it moves through limestone, chalk and dolomite. According to the U.S. Geological Survey, roughly **85% of U.S. homes** have at least moderately hard water. It isn't a health hazard — those minerals are nutrients — but it's expensive.

## How hardness is measured

Hardness is reported as calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) in **mg/L** (same as ppm) or **grains per gallon (gpg)**. One grain per gallon equals **17.1 mg/L**.

| USGS category | mg/L (ppm) | gpg |
|---|---|---|
| Soft | 0–60 | 0–3.5 |
| Moderately hard | 61–120 | 3.5–7 |
| Hard | 121–180 | 7–10.5 |
| Very hard | over 180 | over 10.5 |

Find your number in your utility's water quality report (look for "hardness" or "calcium carbonate"), on our [city lookup](/cities.html), or with a cheap test strip. Convert units with our [hardness converter](/tools.html#hardness).

## What hard water does to your home

- **Water heaters:** scale coats the tank or heat exchanger and insulates the water from the burner or element, so you pay more to heat the same water. Tankless heaters scale up quickly and need regular descaling in hard-water areas.
- **Appliances:** dishwashers, washing machines, coffee makers and kettles wear out faster and need descaling.
- **Fixtures:** clogged showerheads and aerators, crusty faucets, white film on glass shower doors.
- **Soap and detergent:** hard water reacts with soap to form scum, so you use more product to get the same lather.
- **Skin and hair:** many people notice drier skin and duller hair, though effects vary.

## Your options

### 1. Ion-exchange water softener

A tank of resin beads swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium (or potassium). It's the **only proven way to remove hardness** from all the water in your home.

- **Cost:** typically $1,000–$3,000 installed, plus salt and occasional service.
- **Look for:** NSF/ANSI 44 certification.
- **Trade-offs:** adds a small amount of sodium to softened water; needs a drain for regeneration; some communities restrict softeners because of salt discharge. Many owners keep one unsoftened tap for drinking, or add reverse osmosis.

### 2. Salt-free "conditioners" (TAC/NAC)

Template-assisted crystallisation units change minerals into crystals that are less likely to stick to surfaces. They **don't remove hardness**, so you won't get the slippery "soft water" feel, but well-designed units can reduce scale. Look for independent test data rather than marketing claims.

### 3. Magnetic or electronic descalers

Evidence for these is weak and inconsistent. We don't recommend relying on them.

### 4. Point fixes

Shower filters don't soften water. For a renter in a hard-water area, the practical approach is a descaling routine (vinegar or citric acid for kettles and showerheads), a dishwasher rinse aid, and a pitcher or countertop filter if you dislike the taste.

## When does a softener pay off?

Roughly:

- **Under 7 gpg (120 mg/L):** usually not worth it unless you have a tankless water heater or a strong preference.
- **7–10 gpg:** a judgement call — run the numbers.
- **Over 10.5 gpg (very hard):** most homeowners come out ahead over the softener's 10–15 year life, especially larger households.

## Cities with notoriously hard water

Phoenix, Las Vegas, San Antonio, San Diego, Indianapolis, Kansas City, Tampa, Calgary and London are all known for hard to very hard water. Seattle, Portland, Boston, San Francisco, Vancouver and Manchester are famously soft. See every profile on the [city lookup](/cities.html).

## Bottom line

Measure first. If you're in the very hard range and own your home, a certified softener usually pays for itself. If you're moderate, focus on maintenance and target the appliances that suffer most.
